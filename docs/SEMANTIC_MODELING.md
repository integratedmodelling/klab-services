# Semantic Modeling

Science has been [defined](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science) as a process that "builds and organizes knowledge in the form of testable explanations and predictions about the universe". Much scientific activity is aimed at producing informational artifacts that describe the structure and the function of the world around us. Two main types of artifacts are _data_, embodying an "observed" view of the world obtained through measurements, rankings or categorizations, and _models_, embodying a causal hypothesis about how one or more aspects of the world change and evolve. All of these describe, with varying accuracy, the world around us for human perusal, in conformity with one or more, stated or implicit purposes. The job of science is to provide, extend, update and improve these descriptions so that they can best serve the needs of humanity.

Semantic modeling enforces the precise alignment of the _meaning_ of the observed world with the artifacts that science produces to describe it. In current practice, the terminology that describes the day-to-day products of science ("variables", "data" such as maps and tables, "indicators", "models" such as differential equations or computational algorithms) provides the elements of discourse that build the scientific narrative. Using these relatively technical terms creates barriers around the intelligibility and communicability of both problems and solutions, particularly when the dialogue is between linguistically distant communities that need each other's knowledge - such as science and policy. By making semantics the starting and the ending point of a scientific investigation, semantic modeling can help address one of the worst shortcomings of modern science: the "ivory tower" problem of incompatible representations, languages, jargons, methods, scales and, ultimately, communities of practice.

The problems that can be solved through semantically driven integration are currently so unsolved that, for the most part, they barely appear in either the scientific or the policy discourse. In fact they don't even seem to appear as an imperative, utopistic or not, within scientific, engineering or policy goals. The envisioned realm of application of science seems mostly limited to what can be solved effectively without integration - essentially what sits comfortably inside disciplinary boundaries, one language at a time, one brain at a time. While lip service is regularly paid to interdisciplinarity and "open science" in requests for proposals, mission statements and literature, the existing examples of integrated, reusable and interoperable science (the Findable, Accessible, Interoperable and Reusable principles known as FAIR) are limited in scope and vision and nothing shines as a virtuous example to refer to.

Now imagine that the "digital twins"[REF] so actively sought by current policy makers, for contexts ranging from marine jurisdictions to sustainable cities and river basins, were built in a way that would make them capable of: 1) discovering each other and sharing inputs and outputs dynamically, each time choosing the best performing one among all the existing; 2) reconfiguring themselves and adapting their algorithms when new data or better-tested hypotheses come in to explain the phenomena of interest; and 3) being developed and maintained by independent experts who don't _have_ to speak each other's language, or even know of each other, to share their knowledge effectively. The integrated result of this scenario would be a connected, adaptive, dynamic, collaborative, _true_ digital twin that automatically maintains itself and can detect cross-sector interactions and feedbacks that nobody had to explicitly program in. Imagine easy and open access to it, through APIs and applications, for any interested party - citizen science to governments and international governing bodies. The resulting digital ecosystem would be a global, inclusive, democratic _knowledge commons_ that could grow with time, supporting all sectors of society in addressing complex, cross-disciplinary and cross-scale problems unimaginable today. A lofty vision, for sure - but also one that remains well within the scope of current technology. The rest of this document discusses semantic modeling as a practical approach aimed at instrumenting and operationalizing this vision, so that it can be understood, demanded, built and achieved.

## Meaning as the key to knowledge.

k.LAB implements an intelligent modeller whose primary job is to assist scientific activities by responding to a logical query made in a context, producing a scientific observation in return. The query consists of a semantic expression (we call it an _observable query_, or, in short, an _observable_) that expresses _what_ we're after, with no mention of _how_ it should be produced. After choosing a context of observation, we submit an observable and expect, if the query succeeds, to obtain a scientific _observation_ of that observable in that context. In practice, an application or an API will assist you in performing the following steps:

1. define a **context** (space, time, and their representation, all of them optional): say we go all the way and choose a rectangular portion of France, with values seen on a 100m grid, during the year 2015.
2. submit an **observable** as a query: say, `economics:MarketValue of each infrastructure:Building` - which should be self-explanatory, ignoring for now the strange `economics:` and `infrastructure:` prefixes;
3. If everything goes well, k.LAB responds with an **observation**: in our example, because of the context we have chosen, our result will consist of a gridded map with averaged building market values in each 100m^2^ cell, probably complemented by a "folder" of buildings each with their monetary value, the latter courtesy of k.LAB's AI reasoning. Importantly, the observation comes with its _semantics_, i.e. its _meaning_ (the observable we requested) and the _context_ where it was computed. In fact we refer to observations as the result of _contextualizing_ the observable.

The main tenet of semantic modeling is that the key to refer to any knowledge is the observable - a logical expression that _only_ describes its meaning. This means that unless we know better, we do not refer to a _specific_ knowledge artifact (like a file or a URL): rather, we submit our wish to know something, e.g. the height of all buildings in a region, in the same terms that any rational human would use. If we _do_ know better, i.e. we know exactly where to find that knowledge for the context, we _still_ use the meaning, this time to _semantically annotate_ our knowledge, so that others can find it without having to be told a URL or file name. We do that in the specific scope that our information covers, guaranteeing that our result is used exactly where and how we want it to be (more on that later).

Learning how to formulate observable queries is at the heart of k.LAB, and doing so will give you the power of summoning the intelligence of a thousand modelers - even without any knowledge of the specific science behind each observation. That science will be, anyway, fully documented as part of the _provenance_ information linked to the observation. You can use observables to build sophisticated models, but they are flexible enough to be used as on-the-fly semantic queries.

EXAMPLES - observations of countables vs. dependents. Why one produces 0+ observations and the other exactly 1 (the observation of a collective observable vs. an individual where the observation has been instantiated).


Say that you are an urban planner or a concerned citizen, and you want to know which high-value properties are in a flood-prone area, with no pre-existing knowledge about either buildings or floods, but with specific flood probability and property value thresholds in mind. You can submit the following observable through a web application such as the ARIES explorer, combining three concepts with the _semantic operator_ `probability of` and the _value operators_ `whose`, `where`, and `>`)

```kim
    infrastructure:Building
		whose economics:MarketValue > 2000000.EUR@2015
		where probability of earth:Flood > 0.7
```

The response, if successful, will be a collection of building objects, each with their associated market value, that are located wherever the observation of "probability of earth:Flood" has values above 0.75. All the necessary data and models to answer the question will be automatically located by the AI in k.LAB. The result will come with a map of flood probability, necessary to filter the building data. If you want a map of where those buildings are located, you add the `presence of` semantic operator, which _dereifies_ the building observation into a presence/absence value at the resolution chosen for the context:

```
    presence of (infrastructure:Building whose economics:MarketValue > 2000000.EUR@2015)
        where probability of earth:Flood > 0.7
```

This observable will give you a map that has the value "true" wherever your conditions are met. If you have your spatial resolution set to a fine enough value (say 10 m^2^) and data are available somewhere on the network, you will see the individual outlines of the buildings involved. If you want the system to explain `probability of earth:Flood` for the coming week instead of historical data for 2015, set your context's time coverage to next week: if available, the system will pick up predictive flood models that cover the near future in lieu of the 2015-scoped data.

Now, writing these queries may lie beyond the ability of a general user, but using other k.LAB features you can easily write an application where you tie pre-defined observables to user actions, such as buttons or checkboxes in a standard user interface, maybe with user-defined template parameters. Such applications can be served locally or over the web. You can also use the observables to make _models_ that you can use for yourself, or, if you are an expert, contribute to the network for (your or others') queries to find. Say that you know how to make an observation of a particular observable based on other observations: for example

```
model occurrence of agriculture:Pollinator biology:Insect caused by earth:Weather
	observing
		earth:AtmosphericTemperature in Celsius named air_temperature,
		earth:SolarRadiation in J named solar_radiation
	set to [0.62 + 1.027 * air_temperature + 0.006 * solar_radiation);
```

After writing and testing this model and proving that it explains its observable adequately, you can contribute that knowledge through a public project available on the network, adding a new piece to an ever-expanding semantic web that integrates science from all sources. You can also tie your model to a specific context of execution, so that it will not be used to explain the observable outside of some specific contexts and conditions (the model above, incarnating the results of an empirical local study, clearly calls for that). The principle behind all this is that scientists are experts at _something_, not _everything_, and usually their knowledge only applies to well-defined contexts. Using observables as the key to knowledge under a common _worldview_ allows us to codify and reuse any useful scientific result so that they can be found when needed, by humans or by k.LAB-enabled algorithms, enabling the fully integrated, interoperable science that is the motivation behind k.LAB.
